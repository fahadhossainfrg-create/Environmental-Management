Environmental change and management

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

A changing environment

Environmental change is an alteration to the conditions, features or living communities of a place. Some changes are mainly natural: a storm can erode a dune overnight, and a flood can carry sediment downstream and build a floodplain over time. Other changes are driven or intensified by people: clearing vegetation exposes soil to rainfall, burning fossil fuels adds greenhouse gases and building roads changes how water moves. The same place can experience several changes at once.

How geographers investigate change

Begin with what changed, where and over what period. Compare evidence collected at similar times or in similar conditions. A single photograph can show bare soil, but repeated photos or measurements are needed to tell whether erosion is increasing. Then trace causes and impacts at different scales. Clearing one riverbank is a local action, but sediment can move through a whole catchment and reach a coastal estuary.

Managing change

Environmental management involves choices and actions that prevent damage, reduce impacts or restore environments. For a creek, a council might prevent further clearing through planning rules, reduce runoff with vegetated drains and restore damaged banks with native plants. To judge progress, measure bank erosion, water clarity and the survival of plants over several seasons. Different strategies can be combined because they address different parts of the problem.

Three types of management

Prevention stops a damaging activity before it takes place. For example, protecting the last intact wetland in a catchment preserves its water storage and habitat. Mitigation reduces harm from an activity that continues, such as slowing stormwater before it enters a creek. Restoration repairs some functions after damage, such as reconnecting a drained wetland to its floodplain. A restored area may take years to recover, so monitoring needs to continue after the work finishes.

Worked investigation • A changing creek

Imagine two creek reaches with similar slopes and rainfall. One retains native bankside vegetation, while the other was cleared for construction. Repeat photographs and turbidity measurements before and after storms can help test whether clearing increased sediment. Compare the reaches at similar flow levels. A muddy reading after a storm is evidence of sediment in the water. To identify where it came from, examine upstream land use, check construction drains and repeat measurements.

Managing change

Human activities that contribute

Clearing creek banks
Removing roots and ground cover leaves soil exposed. Rain and flowing water can carry sediment into the creek and deposit it farther downstream.
Paving land for roads and buildings
Hard surfaces reduce infiltration and speed up runoff. A creek draining a developed catchment may receive larger pulses of stormwater after rain.
Building on or removing coastal dunes
Construction and vegetation removal can reduce the dune’s ability to trap sand and protect inland areas. A storm can then expose more property to waves and erosion.

Identify the process, the pressure and the scale

A pressure is something that can cause change. An impact is what happens to people, places or ecosystems as a result. The same process can be natural and still be intensified by human activity. For example, a storm can erode a beach naturally. Removing dune vegetation or building too close to the shoreline can make the impact more severe. Look at the timing as well as the location. A storm can shift sand in hours, while a long-term rise in sea level can change how often dunes and buildings are exposed to large waves.

Choose a response and test whether it works

A council could protect existing dunes through development setbacks, reduce trampling by directing visitors onto boardwalks, and restore damaged dunes with suitable native plants. A seawall may protect structures directly behind it, but its effects on beach width and erosion nearby should also be monitored. Residents can stay on marked tracks and report damaged vegetation. Compare beach profiles from fixed survey points before and after storms and check what happened at a similar site. A single photograph of a repaired beach shows how it looked on one day. Repeated measurements show whether the response is holding up.

Environmental worldviews

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

What shapes a decision?

A worldview is a set of beliefs and values that influences how a person or group understands the world. An environmental worldview affects which benefits, costs and responsibilities a person notices. People can agree to protect an environment while giving different reasons for doing so.

Human-centred and Earth-centred views

A human-centred view gives priority to human needs and benefits. A person might support protecting a wetland because it reduces flooding, provides recreation and saves money on water treatment. An Earth-centred view gives weight to the wellbeing of ecosystems and the value of living things in their own right. A person with this view might protect the same wetland because its wildlife and ecological relationships have value even when people gain little direct benefit.

A wetland decision

Imagine a council deciding whether to build a road across a wetland. Some residents emphasise safer travel and shorter trips. Others emphasise breeding habitat, water movement and long-term ecosystem health. A route around the wetland may preserve habitat while still improving travel, but it may cost more. A geographical explanation links each position to a value and shows how that value affects the preferred decision.

How values change a management plan

Consider a proposed dam. A human-centred case might stress reliable water for towns and farms, but it could also oppose the dam if it harms downstream fisheries or displaces households. An Earth-centred case might focus on keeping the river connected so fish can migrate and natural floods can replenish wetlands. A proposal for water recycling could appeal to both groups for different reasons. Look for the reason behind the choice before you label the worldview.

Weighing competing viewpoints

A stakeholder is a person or group affected by a decision or able to influence it. In a wetland decision, Traditional Owners, residents, developers and conservation groups may bring different rights, knowledge and priorities. Ask who receives the benefits, who bears the costs and when those impacts occur. A decision can generate immediate employment while creating long-term habitat loss. An informed judgement considers both time scales and the evidence for each impact.

Perspectives and decisions

Compare the reason behind each decision

A human-centred approach may place high value on jobs, reliable water, recreation or protection from flooding. It can support conservation because healthy environments benefit people now and in the future. An Earth-centred approach may protect habitats and ecological relationships even when no direct benefit to people has been established. A claim that “new technology will solve the problem” shows confidence in technology. Whether the argument is human-centred or Earth-centred also depends on what the technology is meant to protect.

Apply both views to the same management choice

An estuary council could propose a seasonal closure where fish breed. Someone who relies on recreational fishing might support the closure because it helps maintain future catches. A person prioritising the ecosystem might support the same closure because breeding fish contribute to food webs and have value as part of a living system. If a group opposes the closure, consider whether the concern is lost access, weak evidence or an objection to the boundary. Compare the stated reasons rather than assigning a worldview from the preferred action alone. Include the rights and decision-making authority of Traditional Owners where decisions concern Country.

An Earth-centred argument might focus on reconnecting rivers and floodplains so native plants and animals can complete their life cycles. The amount recovered records progress towards a target. Ecological footprints

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Measuring environmental demand

An ecological footprint estimates the biologically productive land and water needed to supply the resources a person or population uses and account for part of the waste it produces, particularly carbon dioxide from fossil fuel use. It is measured in global hectares, a unit adjusted for average biological productivity. Biocapacity is the ability of productive ecosystems to regenerate resources and absorb some wastes. Demand above available biocapacity is called ecological overshoot.

How everyday choices add up

Transport, home energy, food, housing and purchases all involve resources. A car trip can require fuel and add emissions. Food production may need cropland, grazing land or fishing grounds. Imported goods can shift part of a person's demand to other countries. Water use matters to sustainability, but a standard ecological footprint is different from a water footprint or a complete measure of environmental harm.

Comparing Australia and the world

Australia has generally had a higher footprint per person than the world average. Car-dependent travel, energy use and high consumption help explain how a high-income country can have high demand per person. A country's total demand also depends on its population. A high footprint per person and a large total footprint are different findings. In a comparison, use the same year, units and method.

Try a footprint estimate

Give yourself 1, 2 or 3 for each category: travel, household energy, food and purchases. Use 1 for lower demand, 2 for mixed choices and 3 for higher demand. For example, walking most days scores lower for travel than mostly travelling alone by car. Use the result to identify one habit to investigate. This four-part activity is a classroom comparison. A score does not convert to global hectares or a number of Earths.

Read the units before making a comparison

Ecological footprint per person and national ecological footprint answer different questions. In a simplified comparison, a country of 10 million people with a footprint of 6 global hectares per person has a total of 60 million global hectares. A country of 40 million people at 2 global hectares per person has a total of 80 million global hectares. The first country puts greater demand on ecosystems per person. The second has greater total demand because its population is larger. These figures are illustrative.

Why demand can exceed supply

If forest harvest exceeds regrowth for several years, the standing stock can decline. At a global scale, overshoot means total demand exceeds what productive ecosystems can regenerate in the period. A “number of Earths” estimate compares demand with available global biocapacity. It depends on the accounting year and assumptions. Improvements in electricity generation can lower the carbon component, while public transport, compact urban design and longer-lasting products can change the demand associated with everyday choices.

Reducing demand

Human activities that contribute

Using petrol cars and fossil-fuel electricity
Burning fuel releases carbon dioxide, which contributes to the carbon component of an ecological footprint. Longer journeys or more energy use can increase that demand.
Buying and discarding more food or clothing
Growing crops, grazing livestock and making textiles use productive land and energy. Throwing products away early can increase the need for replacement production.
Expanding housing and transport infrastructure
Buildings and roads occupy land. Development patterns can also make car travel more common, adding demand beyond the area physically built on.

What can make a footprint larger?

A footprint can include demand for cropland, grazing land, fishing grounds, forest products, built-up land and the biologically productive area associated with absorbing some carbon emissions. A household’s choices about transport, diet, home energy, packaging and durable goods can affect more than one part of the account. Household water use still matters to rivers and reservoirs. A standard ecological footprint does not directly measure litres of water used, local water scarcity or the harm caused by a particular pollutant.

Act at household, school and government scales

A household can plan meals to reduce wasted food, repair items and use public transport where practical. A school could compare how often disposable containers are used before and after introducing refill stations or reusable lunch containers. Government decisions about public transport, energy supply and building efficiency affect the options available to many people at once. Track the specific behaviour that changed, such as the number of car journeys or the mass of edible food discarded. A short lifestyle quiz can prompt a discussion, but it cannot convert a few choices into a reliable estimate of how many Earths are required.

Real-world data

Australia and the world in 2026

Global Footprint Network estimated that humanity’s ecological demand in 2026 was equivalent to 1.73 Earths, with global Earth Overshoot Day falling on 30 July. In its country comparison, demand would be equivalent to 4.9 Earths if everyone lived like the average Australian resident, with a hypothetical date of 16 March. Both figures compare demand with global regenerative capacity. They are accounting estimates, and revisions to methods or data can move the dates.

The carbon cycle

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Carbon moves through four spheres

The atmosphere stores carbon in gases such as carbon dioxide. The biosphere stores carbon in organisms. The hydrosphere stores carbon in ocean and freshwater systems, including dissolved forms. The lithosphere stores carbon in rocks, sediments, fossil fuels and the solid material of soils. Soil organic matter links living processes to the land store.

Fast and slow transfers

Plants and algae take in carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and build sugars. Feeding moves carbon through food webs. Plants, animals and microbes release carbon dioxide through respiration. Decomposers break down dead material, returning some carbon to the air while some remains in soils. Carbon dioxide also moves between the atmosphere and oceans. Some carbon is stored for much longer in marine sediments, rocks or fossil fuel deposits.

Humans change the balance

Burning coal, oil and gas moves long-stored carbon into the atmosphere quickly. Deforestation removes trees that would otherwise take up carbon and can release carbon stored in wood and soils. Oceans absorb some of the extra carbon dioxide. When it dissolves, seawater pH falls and carbonate becomes less available to shell-building organisms and some corals. This process is ocean acidification. It differs from coral bleaching caused by heat stress.

Stores, flows, sources and sinks

A store holds carbon, while a flow transfers it between stores. A carbon sink absorbs more carbon than it releases over a given period. A forest can gain carbon as its trees grow, but a severe fire can make it a net source for a time. The same ecosystem can alternate between the two depending on rainfall, growth, fire and land use. Respiration continues when photosynthesis slows or stops, so the net exchange is the balance of several processes.

Follow one carbon atom

Imagine a carbon atom in atmospheric carbon dioxide. A leaf absorbs the gas, and the plant uses the carbon to make a sugar. A herbivore eats the leaf and releases some of the carbon as carbon dioxide during respiration. Carbon in uneaten leaves can move into the soil when they fall. In a different pathway, carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater, where carbon can remain dissolved, enter marine organisms or eventually reach seabed sediments.

Carbon management

Human activities that contribute

Burning coal, oil or gas
Power stations, vehicles and industry can transfer carbon stored underground into the atmosphere as carbon dioxide.
Clearing and burning vegetation
Fire and decomposition can release carbon from plants and soils. Fewer trees remain to take up carbon dioxide through photosynthesis.
Making cement
Heating limestone releases carbon dioxide from the rock during production. Burning fuel to heat the kiln can add further emissions.

How carbon moves on different time scales

Photosynthesis, respiration and decomposition can transfer carbon over days, seasons or years. Some dead organic material becomes part of the soil, where carbon may remain for years or much longer depending on conditions. Sediments and some rocks store carbon on much longer time scales. Burning fossil fuels transfers carbon from a long-term geological store to the atmosphere quickly. Compare the size of a carbon store with the rate of transfer. A large store can still release only a small amount each year.

Manage both emissions and existing stores

Replacing some fossil-fuel electricity with lower-emissions sources reduces a transfer of carbon into the atmosphere. Protecting an intact forest can avoid emissions from clearing and preserve the forest’s carbon store. Restoring native vegetation can increase uptake as plants grow, but growth takes time and drought or fire can reverse some gains. Reducing carbon dioxide emissions also addresses the cause of ocean acidification. A shell-building organism may struggle to form its shell when there is less carbonate available, even though seawater generally remains alkaline. A falling pH means the water has become more acidic than it was before.

Real-world data

Carbon dioxide in the global atmosphere

The global monthly average measured across NOAA’s marine surface sites was 424.09 parts per million (ppm) in July 2025 and 426.10 ppm in July 2026. The increase between those two months was 2.01 ppm. Comparing the same month helps reduce the effect of seasonal differences. A long series is still needed to separate long-term growth from year-to-year changes in emissions and the carbon absorbed by land and oceans.

Climate change

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The enhanced greenhouse effect

Weather describes short-term conditions. Climate describes patterns over many years. Greenhouse gases naturally keep Earth warm enough for life by absorbing and re-emitting some outgoing infrared energy. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide, and activities such as livestock farming and waste decomposition can add methane. Higher greenhouse gas concentrations intensify the greenhouse effect, raising average temperatures.

What changes as the climate warms?

Heatwaves can become more frequent or intense. Warmer air can hold more water vapour, increasing the potential for heavy rain in some situations, while some regions experience drier conditions or longer droughts. Impacts vary by region and hazard. Sea level rises as warmer seawater expands and land-based ice melts, increasing risks to low coastal communities. Ocean heat can also expose coral reefs to bleaching.

Mitigation and adaptation

Mitigation tackles the cause by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or increasing carbon storage. Renewable energy, energy-efficient buildings and forest protection can contribute. Adaptation reduces harm from impacts. Shade trees, heat plans and flood-resilient planning are examples. A council could do both by making public buildings more energy efficient and using cool spaces to support residents during heatwaves.

How evidence shows a changing climate

Scientists compare long records from weather stations, ocean measurements and satellites. A long record helps separate a trend from year-to-year variability. Changes in air temperature, ocean heat, sea level and ice can support the same conclusion through different lines of evidence. A specific flood also depends on rainfall intensity, soil saturation, drainage and what has been built on the floodplain, so describing the long-term trend and explaining an individual event are different tasks.

Compare two responses to coastal risk

A seawall can reduce wave damage to buildings directly behind it, but it can be costly and may alter erosion nearby. Avoiding new development in areas likely to flood reduces future exposure, but it does less for homes already built there. Dune restoration can buffer some wave energy where there is sufficient space and sand. A defensible response explains which people and places it protects, when it will work and what risks remain.

Responses to climate risks

Human activities that contribute

Generating power from fossil fuels and burning fuel for transport
Coal- and gas-fired power stations, petrol cars and diesel trucks release carbon dioxide. Accumulated emissions strengthen the greenhouse effect.
Clearing forests for farming or development
Clearing can release stored carbon and remove vegetation that would otherwise continue taking up carbon dioxide.
Raising cattle and sheep or sending food waste to landfill
Ruminant digestion and the decomposition of food in low-oxygen landfill conditions release methane, another greenhouse gas.

Causes, hazards and exposure

Burning coal, oil and gas increases atmospheric carbon dioxide. Clearing vegetation can release stored carbon and reduce future uptake. Livestock and some waste processes release methane. Extra greenhouse gases change Earth’s energy balance. The impacts depend on local conditions. For example, hot weather and dry fuels can increase fire danger, but whether a bushfire occurs also depends on ignition, weather and land conditions. Flood losses can increase when buildings occupy flood-prone areas even if the flood hazard itself is unchanged.

Combine mitigation with adaptation

Cleaner electricity, efficient buildings, better public transport and protecting carbon-rich ecosystems can reduce emissions or maintain carbon stores. These are mitigation techniques. Heat warning systems, shade, revised planning rules and flood-ready infrastructure reduce exposure or vulnerability. These are adaptation techniques. For an urban heat plan, plant trees where pedestrians wait or walk, provide shaded places and compare daytime temperatures and shade coverage across neighbourhoods. Trees take time to mature and require maintenance and water, so immediate protection may also require shelters and heat-response plans.

Wildlife exploitation

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

When use becomes unsustainable

Wildlife exploitation occurs when people take or trade wild organisms for food, income, pets or products. Use becomes unsustainable when removal is faster than populations can recover. Illegal trafficking can target animals for skins, ivory or the pet trade. Overfishing can remove breeding adults faster than young fish mature. Habitat loss from farming or urban growth can intensify these pressures by shrinking the areas where wildlife breeds and feeds.

Why the effects spread

Smaller populations can become isolated, lose genetic diversity and face a higher risk of extinction. Removing a predator or major herbivore can change the populations of other species. Loss of pollinators or seed dispersers can also reduce plant reproduction. A change that starts with one species can alter how a whole ecosystem functions.

What makes some species more vulnerable?

Species that grow slowly, mature late or produce few young often recover more slowly after heavy harvesting. Taking breeding adults can reduce future recruitment, which is the addition of young individuals to the population. Catch records can hide a decline if fishers travel farther or use better equipment to maintain the same catch. Comparing catch per unit of effort, age or size of animals, and independent surveys gives a fuller picture.

Reducing bycatch while protecting livelihoods

A fishing community might change where or when it fishes to avoid times when non-target animals are common. Gear designed to let some animals escape can address bycatch while allowing fishing to continue. Monitoring should record catches of both target and non-target species. If protected areas simply shift fishing to a nearby sensitive area, the wider environmental benefit may be smaller than local records suggest.

Protecting wildlife populations

Human activities that contribute

Fishing faster than a stock can recover
Repeatedly removing large numbers of fish, including breeding adults, can reduce the number left to reproduce and rebuild the population.
Using fishing gear that catches non-target animals
Nets or lines can unintentionally catch species such as turtles, seabirds or other fish. This is bycatch, and its effect depends on the gear, place and species.
Capturing or killing wildlife for illegal trade
Demand for pets, skins or other wildlife products can drive removals from wild populations. Fragmented or slow-breeding populations can take longer to recover.

How exploitation interacts with other pressures

Illegal trade may target particular species for pets, skin, decorative products or other uses. Commercial harvest can also be legal yet unsustainable if breeding populations fall faster than they can recover. A species already affected by habitat loss may be especially vulnerable to harvesting. Removing adults can reduce the number of young entering the population. When a seed disperser, pollinator or predator declines, the consequences may also affect species that people did not target.

Match each technique to the threat

Catch limits control how many animals may be taken, minimum size rules can help protect smaller animals and seasonal closures can protect breeding periods. Selective fishing gear and changes in where fishing takes place can reduce bycatch. Protected areas can conserve feeding and breeding habitat. Trade enforcement and consumer education address illegal demand and trafficking. Each needs monitoring. Compare the abundance and size of target animals, record non-target catch, and check whether fishing pressure moved outside the protected area. Recovery may take longer for species that mature slowly.

Deforestation

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Why forests are cleared

Deforestation is the long-term conversion of forest to another land use. Forests can be cleared for cattle, crops, housing, roads or mines. Logging can degrade a forest by removing trees and damaging habitat. Whether it leads to deforestation depends on whether forest cover recovers or the land is permanently converted.

Effects on land, water and climate

Clearing reduces habitat and can separate the remaining patches, making it harder for wildlife to move and find mates. Removing canopy and roots exposes soil to erosion. Sediment can move into streams and reduce water quality. Clearing and burning can also release carbon and reduce future uptake. These effects can extend beyond the cleared area.

Compare forest area with forest condition

A satellite image can show changes in the area covered by trees. It may be harder to tell whether a patch is an intact native forest, regrowth or a single-species plantation. Forest condition also includes species diversity, the presence of old trees and whether habitat patches are connected. A landscape may gain tree cover while remaining less suitable for species that depend on native forest features such as large tree hollows.

A chain of effects across a catchment

Imagine road construction opening access to a forest. Some forest is cleared for buildings and the road splits the remaining habitat. Runoff from exposed ground enters a stream after storms, carrying sediment downstream. Forest loss therefore affects both terrestrial habitat and aquatic conditions. Effective management can combine limits on further clearing, erosion control during construction and restoration of connections between remaining patches.

Preventing and repairing forest loss

Human activities that contribute

Converting forests to pasture or crops
Trees are removed to make room for livestock or planting. The original forest may be permanently replaced by another land use.
Building roads, housing or mines
Infrastructure can remove forest directly and divide the remaining habitat into smaller patches. New roads can also make previously remote areas easier to clear.
Harvesting timber without allowing recovery
Logging can degrade forest structure even if some trees remain. It becomes deforestation when the forest is permanently converted to another land use.

Distinguish the drivers and impacts

Clearing for cattle, crops, housing, roads or mining can permanently convert forest to another land use. Timber harvest can damage remaining trees or soils even where the area later regenerates. Roads can fragment habitat and create exposed edges with different light, wind and temperature. After rain, erosion can carry sediment from a cleared slope into a creek. These changes can occur together, so compare both forest area and condition when describing the impact.

Protect forests, reconnect habitat and reduce demand

Land-use controls can prevent clearing in sensitive places. Where timber harvesting is permitted, keeping stream buffers, retaining habitat trees and limiting damage to the forest floor can reduce some impacts. Habitat corridors help connect isolated patches. Restoring a site with a mix of native species can improve cover and connectivity over time. Reusing timber and recycling paper can reduce demand for some new material. A plantation can supply timber, but its trees may be a similar age and lack the hollows and diversity of a mature native forest. Check canopy cover, native species and connectivity separately.

Net forest loss in 2015–2025 was about 4.12 million hectares per year because forest expansion offset part of the clearing. New forest area can increase total tree cover while the loss of mature native forest still reduces habitat quality and biodiversity.

Water scarcity

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

When freshwater cannot meet demand

Water scarcity occurs when available, usable freshwater is insufficient for people, industries, farming or ecosystems. Physical scarcity can result from low rainfall, drought or overuse. Scarcity can also reflect limited infrastructure, unsafe water or the cost of treatment and delivery. A community can live near a river and still lack safe water if the river is polluted or treatment is inadequate.

Competition within a catchment

Agriculture needs water for crops and livestock. Towns need reliable supplies for drinking, hygiene and services. Rivers and wetlands need enough flow to maintain habitats. During drought, continued extraction can shrink wetlands, concentrate pollutants and leave irrigators with less water. Crop yields can fall and communities can face restrictions or higher costs.

Supply, demand and unequal access

Rainfall is uneven across space and seasons. Groundwater and reservoirs can store water from wetter periods, but groundwater falls when extraction exceeds recharge and reservoirs hold less water during extended drought. Water insecurity also depends on access. A town with limited treatment capacity may have a less reliable supply than a nearby city even when both are close to the same river. Availability, quality and affordability each shape water security.

A drought management example

A catchment authority could reduce irrigation allocations and reserve flows for important wetlands. This can protect drinking-water supplies and habitat, but farmers may face lower production or higher costs. Recycled water may help irrigate parks or supply industry where it is suitably treated. Compare strategies using how much usable water they provide or save, how quickly they can work, their ongoing costs and who is affected by the trade-offs.

Water management techniques

Human activities that contribute

Increasing water extraction for irrigation, industry or growing towns
Demand can outpace river flow, reservoir inflow or groundwater recharge. The risk is greater when rainfall and storage are already low.
Polluting rivers or aquifers
Runoff, sewage or industrial contamination can make water unsuitable for some uses or increase treatment costs, reducing usable supply.
Underinvesting in treatment and distribution
Leaking pipes or inadequate treatment can leave households with an unreliable or unsafe supply even when there is water nearby.

Trace impacts from households to ecosystems

Physical scarcity can arise during drought or when demand grows faster than available supplies. Access problems can occur even where water exists if treatment, pipes or affordability are inadequate. Untreated water can expose people to disease. Reduced irrigation can cut crop yields, increase costs or change what farmers grow. Low river flows can shrink habitat and concentrate some pollutants. A supply plan needs to consider households, industry, agriculture and the needs of rivers and wetlands.

Compare demand measures and new supplies

Fixing leaks can save treated water before it is lost. Efficient irrigation can reduce losses at farm scale, but the benefit to a river depends on whether total extraction also falls. Recycled water can serve suitable uses such as irrigation or industry after appropriate treatment. Desalination can supply coastal cities independently of rainfall, but it requires energy and produces brine that must be managed. Environmental flow rules protect water for river processes. Compare changes in total extraction, reliability, water quality, operating costs and flows available to ecosystems.

Plastic waste and recycling

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

From everyday use to long-term pollution

Single-use packaging is useful for a short time, yet much of it persists for years when littered. Wind and stormwater move light plastic from streets into rivers and the ocean. Wildlife can become entangled or mistake pieces for food. Larger items can fragment into microplastics. Litter also creates clean-up costs and reduces the appeal of beaches and waterways.

Who can act?

Individuals can reuse containers, sort correctly and keep litter out of drains. Councils can provide clear instructions, separate collection and stormwater litter traps. Governments can require producers to help manage packaging, restrict selected single-use items and support container return schemes. Track litter counts and recycling contamination to see whether a strategy works.

Why an item may be hard to recycle

Recycling depends on how a product is made and whether the local system can collect and sort it. A rigid bottle made mostly from one material can be easier to sort than a wrapper that combines several thin layers. Food residue and incorrect items can contaminate a bin load. Some specialised products can be accepted through separate collections. A recycling symbol or a claim on packaging does not determine whether an item belongs in a particular council bin.

Trace plastic from the street to the sea

After a storm, a piece of packaging can wash from a footpath into a drain, then enter a creek and an estuary. It may be trapped by vegetation, carried out with the tide or broken into smaller pieces. A gross pollutant trap can collect some larger litter before it reaches a waterway, but it needs maintenance and cannot capture every small fragment. Reducing litter at the source and intercepting it along the pathway address different stages.

Waste prevention and sorting

Human activities that contribute

Using disposable packaging for short-lived tasks
Takeaway containers, wrappers and shopping bags become waste after brief use. Heavy reliance on single-use items increases the amount that needs collection.
Littering or leaving waste unsecured
Wind and rain can move light plastic from streets and open bins into stormwater drains, rivers and the ocean.
Putting plastic into the wrong collection stream
Soft film or food-contaminated items can interfere with sorting. When local systems cannot collect or reprocess a material, more may go to landfill.

Follow the waste hierarchy

Avoid unnecessary packaging first. Reuse durable items where they can be used repeatedly. Recycle materials accepted by the local service so they can be reprocessed. Disposal is the last option when suitable recovery is unavailable. Clean-ups remove some existing litter, while litter traps can intercept larger items on the way to waterways. These actions address different stages of the problem. Plastic labelled biodegradable or compostable may need particular conditions and can contaminate the wrong collection stream.

Practise sorting a NSW lunchbox

An empty aluminium drink can and clean paper generally belong in the yellow-lid recycling stream if accepted locally. An eligible drink container may instead go to container return. Soft wrapping film generally stays out of yellow-lid bins. Food scraps may go to a food-organics service if the local council accepts them. Batteries and e-waste need dedicated collection. Avoid placing batteries in household bins. To compare results at school, record the number or proportion of incorrect items in recycling bins before and after adding clearer signs, using the same sampling method each time.

Why edible food is thrown away

Food can be lost on farms, in shops and in homes. Produce may be rejected for its shape or appearance. Shops can order more than they sell, and households may forget leftovers or buy larger quantities than needed. Food waste also wastes the water, land, energy and labour used to produce it. What happens to disposable packaging?

Fast fashion and discarded clothing

Donated items may be sold, reused, exported, recycled or discarded depending on their quality and local demand. Prevent edible food waste through planning and stock rotation. Redistribute safe surplus food where practical and compost suitable scraps when prevention is no longer possible. Conduct a simple waste audit

A product’s impacts begin before disposal

Caring for Country and Sea Country

Country and responsibility

For many Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Country and Sea Country encompass land, waters, living things, ancestry, knowledge and relationships. Caring for Country is a continuing responsibility. Knowledge and authority belong to particular peoples and places, and practices vary across Australia. First Nations relationships to Country cannot be reduced to one universal worldview.

Cultural burning and seasonal knowledge

Cultural burning is Indigenous-led fire management guided by knowledge of local Country. At Wattleridge Indigenous Protected Area in northern NSW, Banbai rangers have used seasonal observations to guide burning, including what plants and animals are doing. Carefully planned burns can create a patchwork of burnt and unburnt areas, reduce some fuels and support species adapted to particular fire patterns. Outcomes depend on Country, timing, weather and the needs of different species. Burning is carried out by authorised people with local knowledge.

Harvesting and caring for water

Selective harvesting can leave enough mature plants, seeds or breeding animals to support future populations. Seasonal knowledge can indicate when food is available and which water places require care. Protecting waterholes and using water sources in ways that match seasonal supply helps preserve their function. Ranger programs can combine knowledge held by Traditional Owners with tools such as mapping and repeated species surveys, with decisions made through appropriate local authority.

Why local knowledge matters

Seasonal calendars can draw on indicators such as flowering, animal movements, winds and rainfall. These observations tell people about conditions in a particular place. A seasonal indicator in one region may be absent or occur at another time elsewhere. The Banbai example shows how observations made on Country can inform decisions. It is also an example of a community applying its own authority and knowledge to contemporary management.

Managing across land and water

Sea Country management can involve caring for coastal habitats and monitoring species, pollution and changes in water quality. Work upstream may benefit coastal habitats because rivers carry sediment and nutrients to the sea. A program’s success can include ecological outcomes, such as healthier habitat, as well as cultural outcomes identified by Traditional Owners, such as passing knowledge between generations. The people responsible for Country should guide how knowledge is shared.

Marine environments and human impacts

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Different habitats, connected waters

Marine environments include open oceans, coral reefs, seagrass meadows and coasts. Estuaries are transition areas where river freshwater mixes with seawater, so salinity varies. Phytoplankton, algae and seagrasses support food webs. Reefs provide shelter to fish and invertebrates. Seagrass supports animals such as dugongs and can trap sediment and store carbon. Coastal habitats support fisheries, recreation and tourism, and reefs can reduce some incoming wave energy.

Human pressures

Overfishing can shrink target populations. Bycatch is the unintended capture of non-target organisms, such as turtles, seabirds or dolphins. Some fishing equipment can also damage the seabed. Oil spills can coat or poison organisms. Runoff can carry fertiliser, pesticides and sediment to the sea. Excess nutrients may promote algal growth, and sediment can reduce light for seagrass and coral.

How habitats work together

Some marine animals use different places during their lives. Young fish may find shelter in a mangrove creek or seagrass meadow and later move to reefs or deeper waters. Losing a nursery habitat can therefore affect fish catches or reef food webs elsewhere. Currents also carry nutrients, larvae and pollutants between habitats. This is why managers sometimes protect a connected group of habitats across an estuary or coastal region.

Compare three different interventions

A no-take zone reduces direct harvesting inside a defined area. Changes in fishing gear can reduce bycatch even where fishing continues. Vegetated strips on farms can reduce some sediment before it reaches streams. Each addresses a different mechanism. To measure results, compare fish sizes or abundance for fishing restrictions, non-target catch for gear changes, and downstream turbidity for erosion controls.

Protecting connected marine habitats

Human activities that contribute

Overfishing or using gear that damages the seabed
Removing too many fish changes food webs. Some gear can disturb bottom habitats, while nets or lines may accidentally catch non-target wildlife.
Anchoring in seagrass or modifying the coast
Anchors can uproot seagrass. Dredging and construction can disturb habitat and increase suspended sediment where works take place.
Sending pollution downstream from farms, streets and industry
Runoff can carry sediment, nutrients or litter into estuaries and coastal waters. Oil spills can coat and harm marine animals.

Why these environments matter

Oceans, reefs, estuaries and seagrass meadows support organisms such as phytoplankton, algae, crustaceans, fish, corals, dugongs and other marine mammals. Fisheries provide food and livelihoods, and some marine organisms are studied for compounds that may be useful in medicine. Sand and gravel can be used in construction where extraction is permitted, but removal may disturb habitats and sediment movement. Reefs, mangroves and vegetated shorelines can reduce wave energy and help protect coasts. Their effect depends on local conditions and habitat health.

Reduce pressure at the source and at sea

Catch rules and selective gear can limit overfishing and bycatch. No-anchoring areas and moorings can reduce damage to seagrass and reefs. Prevention and spill-response plans address oil pollution. Catchment buffers, erosion controls and better fertiliser management reduce land-based inputs. Cleaner energy helps address marine warming and ocean acidification over the longer term. Use different measures to judge different interventions. Track non-target catch for a gear change, turbidity for runoff controls, and seagrass cover where anchoring rules have changed.

Coral bleaching

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Causes, impacts and management

Human activities that contribute

Burning fossil fuels for electricity, transport and industry
Carbon dioxide emissions warm the climate and oceans. This raises the risk of marine heatwaves, the main trigger of mass bleaching on reefs such as the Great Barrier Reef.
Clearing forests or burning vegetation during land conversion
Carbon released from vegetation and reduced future uptake can add to atmospheric carbon dioxide. The effect on reefs is indirect, through climate and ocean warming.
Clearing land, disturbing soil or applying excess fertiliser in reef catchments
Runoff can lower water quality on some inshore reefs and add to coral stress or slow recovery. It is a separate local pressure from the heat driving widespread bleaching.

What triggers bleaching?

Marine heatwaves are the main trigger of mass bleaching. Rising greenhouse gas concentrations warm the ocean and make damaging heat stress more likely. The intensity and duration of high temperatures both matter. During heat stress, the relationship between coral and its microscopic algal partners breaks down. Healthy reef-building corals are animals. Their algal partners, often called zooxanthellae, use photosynthesis to supply much of the coral’s energy. During bleaching the coral loses many algae or their pigments, so the white skeleton is visible through living tissue. Some species are more sensitive than others, and local depth, currents and light can affect exposure. Intense sunlight can intensify heat stress. Sudden cold, prolonged exposure at unusually low tides, or changes in salinity from freshwater flooding can also cause bleaching in particular places. Ocean acidification is a separate pressure that can make it harder for some corals to build skeletons.

What happens to wildlife, people and the reef?

Bleached coral can recover if conditions improve before the colony dies and algal partners return. Severe or repeated bleaching can reduce growth and reproduction, increase disease risk and cause mortality. When living coral declines, fish that depend on it can lose food or shelter. As dead reef structures erode, some habitats may become less complex and may absorb less wave energy. The consequences can include changes to fishing, tourism and cultural uses of Sea Country. The impacts differ between places and depend on how much coral survives and how quickly the reef recovers.

Management techniques and their limits

Cutting greenhouse gas emissions addresses the main driver of mass bleaching by limiting further warming. Local measures can help stressed reefs recover. Catchment managers can reduce sediment and nutrient runoff through better fertiliser management, erosion controls and stream vegetation. Reef managers can control crown-of-thorns starfish at priority sites to reduce predation, and use zoning or moorings to limit some local disturbance. These measures relieve additional pressures while the coral recovers. They cannot cool an ocean-wide marine heatwave. At selected sites, coral nurseries or larval restoration trials can help test recovery techniques, but they are limited by cost, scale and the conditions corals face after restoration.

Detect heat stress and measure recovery

Satellite monitoring can show where unusually warm sea temperatures have accumulated. Degree Heating Weeks combine the intensity and duration of recent heat stress to flag where bleaching risk is rising. Divers and other survey teams can record the proportion of colonies that are pale or bleached, then revisit the same sites to track survival and living coral cover. Bleaching observations during a heatwave and mortality measured later answer different questions. Compare the same reefs or regions over time and consider other events such as cyclones or starfish outbreaks. AIMS reported that northern hard coral cover fell from 39.8% in 2024 to 30.0% in 2025 after mass bleaching and other disturbances. It rose to 35.1% in 2026. Recovery was uneven across the reef, and northern cover had not returned to its 2024 level.

Great Barrier Reef and crown-of-thorns starfish

ReadingManagementReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The reef and the pressures it faces

The Great Barrier Reef is a network of coral reefs, seagrass meadows and other marine habitats off Queensland’s east coast. Coral reefs provide food and shelter for marine life. The reef is part of Traditional Owners’ Sea Country, which they continue to care for.

Several pressures can affect the same reef during its recovery. For example, a cyclone can break coral, a marine heatwave can cause bleaching, and crown-of-thorns starfish can eat some of the coral that survives.

How a starfish outbreak develops

Adult crown-of-thorns starfish are native predators that feed on living coral tissue. At low numbers, their feeding is part of the reef food web. During an outbreak, there are enough starfish to eat coral faster than it can grow back.

Young starfish, called larvae, drift in the water before settling. Currents can carry larvae from one reef to another. How many survive depends on factors such as food supply and where currents carry them. Nutrients can also reach the reef naturally when deep ocean water rises to the surface.

How human activities add pressure

A catchment is an area of land that drains to a river or another waterway. During heavy rain, runoff from reef catchments can carry nutrients and sediment into coastal waters. The effects vary with rainfall, currents and how far the material travels.

Applying fertiliser to farmland
Rain can wash excess nutrients into rivers and out to sea. Under suitable conditions, these nutrients can increase the growth of tiny floating algae that starfish larvae eat. More food may help more larvae survive and increase the chance of an outbreak.
Clearing vegetation or leaving soil bare
Rain can wash loose soil into waterways. The sediment can make coastal water cloudy, reducing the light that corals and seagrass need to grow.
Burning fossil fuels
Greenhouse gas emissions warm the ocean. Marine heatwaves can bleach corals and slow recovery, leaving reefs to cope with heat stress as well as coral loss from starfish feeding.

How managers protect coral

  1. Survey reefs. Teams measure starfish density, which is the number of starfish in a given area, and estimate how much of the reef is covered by living coral.
  2. Prioritise reefs. Managers use the surveys to decide where culling can protect the most vulnerable coral or help limit the spread of an outbreak.
  3. Cull starfish. Trained divers remove starfish from selected reefs. Fewer starfish feeding on coral gives it a better chance to regrow.
  4. Return and monitor. Teams revisit reefs to check whether starfish numbers are rising again and whether coral cover is improving.

Culling reduces predation on selected reefs. Other measures address pressures across the reef system. Farmers can match fertiliser use to crop needs and keep vegetation along waterways to reduce runoff. Cutting greenhouse gas emissions helps limit long-term ocean warming.

How to judge whether control worked

Starfish culled measures how much control work was done. To assess the results, compare starfish density and live coral cover using the same survey method over time. Falling starfish density and rising coral cover would support the conclusion that control helped.

Geographers can strengthen that conclusion by comparing similar reefs and checking when other disturbances occurred. A cyclone or heatwave can reduce coral cover even after divers have successfully reduced starfish density.

Ecosystems, food webs and trophic cascades

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

The parts of an ecosystem

An ecosystem includes organisms and the physical conditions with which they interact. Biotic factors are living components such as plants, predators and bacteria. Abiotic factors include sunlight, temperature, salinity, soil and water availability. Changing one factor can change how other parts of the system function.

Feeding relationships

Producers, such as plants and phytoplankton, capture energy through photosynthesis. Primary consumers eat producers, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and tertiary consumers can feed on secondary consumers. Fungi and bacteria decompose dead material and release nutrients. Detritivores such as earthworms help break material into smaller pieces. Food chains show one route for energy. Food webs show several connected routes. In an energy-flow diagram, draw arrows from the food to the organism that eats it.

A chain reaction across feeding levels

A trophic cascade is an indirect change across feeding levels. If a predator declines, its herbivore prey may increase or spend more time feeding in exposed areas. More grazing can reduce plants. The size of the effect depends on other predators, the availability of food and local conditions. A change beginning with producers can also move up through feeding levels.

Energy moves and nutrients cycle

Producers use sunlight to make organic matter. Consumers obtain energy when they feed, and organisms use much of that energy for movement, growth and other life processes. Less usable energy is available at higher feeding levels, which is one reason many ecosystems support fewer large predators than primary consumers. Nutrients released during decomposition can return to soil or water and be taken up again by producers.

Compare changes from the top and bottom

A top-down change starts with a consumer. If a predator declines, grazing by its prey may rise and reduce plants. A bottom-up change starts with resource supply or producers. If a drought reduces grass growth, herbivores may have less food and predator numbers may eventually decline. A food web shows alternative pathways, so the exact effect depends on whether organisms can switch to other foods.

How ecosystems respond

Human activities that contribute

Hunting or deliberately removing predators
When predator numbers fall, their prey may become more numerous or feed in different places. Increased grazing can reduce some plants and the resources available to other species.
Introducing or reintroducing a species
Adding a predator or competitor can change which species survive, what they eat and where they feed. The direction and size of the change depend on the local food web.
Building dams or clearing vegetation
Dams can change flow and access to habitat. Clearing removes producers and shelter. Either can change feeding relationships, although a trophic cascade requires evidence of effects across feeding levels.

Read the roles in a food web

A biotic factor is a living part of the ecosystem, such as a plant, animal, fungus or bacterium. An abiotic factor is a non-living condition, such as sunlight, temperature, rainfall, salinity or soil. Producers capture energy through photosynthesis. Primary consumers eat producers, and higher-level consumers feed on other consumers. An omnivore can feed at more than one trophic level. Fungi and bacteria break down dead material and return nutrients to the environment. Use arrows from the food to the organism receiving its energy.

Work through a change at more than one level

If predators are removed, their prey may increase in number or spend longer feeding in places they previously avoided. This can increase grazing pressure on producers. A change in vegetation can then affect other animals that use it for food or shelter. Effects can also start at the bottom: a drought can reduce plant growth, leaving less food for herbivores and indirectly affecting predators. Environmental changes such as dams or clearing can alter abiotic factors and feeding relationships at the same time. To test a cascade, look for changes across several trophic levels and check whether other factors explain the pattern.

Yellowstone and the return of wolves

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Why wolves matter in Yellowstone

Yellowstone National Park in the United States contains river valleys, forests and open grasslands. Wolves were eliminated from the park in the early twentieth century and reintroduced in 1995 and 1996. For years, heavy browsing by elk affected woody plants in parts of the northern range. When wolves returned, they hunted elk and could also affect where elk fed or how long they stayed in some places.

Tracing indirect effects

Reduced browsing can allow willow or aspen to grow taller where water and other conditions are suitable. Willows provide food and building material for beavers, while shrubs give birds shelter and nesting sites. Beaver dams can slow water and create ponds and wetlands used by fish, amphibians and birds. Vegetation and wetlands can stabilise some banks and alter local water flow. These links illustrate how an effect can extend beyond wolves and elk.

Why recovery varies

River flow, water tables, climate, hunting outside the park and other predators also influence elk and vegetation. Wolves can affect coyotes, which can influence small-mammal prey, but changes in rabbit or rodent numbers vary by place and time. Evidence for a cascade is stronger when changes are observed at several feeding levels and plausible alternative causes are considered.

Draw the links before making a prediction

Start with grass and willow as producers. Draw arrows from these plants to elk, then from elk to wolves. Add willow to beavers and seeds to small rodents, then add small rodents to coyotes. This shows why a food web is more informative than a single chain. For example, a change in elk browsing can affect animals that rely on the same plants even when those animals never interact directly with elk.

How to test the explanation

A study could compare elk browsing, willow height and local water levels at multiple sites before and after wolf reintroduction. Recovery in moist sites with low browsing would support a role for both factors. If willow grows only where water is available, a fall in browsing may be insufficient on its own. Evidence from several places and several links in the chain is stronger than a single before-and-after photograph.

Evidence of a trophic cascade

Human activities that contribute

Removing wolves through predator control
People deliberately reduced wolf numbers before wolves disappeared from Yellowstone. Without that predator, elk faced less predation from wolves, and heavy browsing limited young woody plants at some sites.
Reintroducing wolves to the park
People restored a predator–prey relationship. In some places, changes in elk numbers or behaviour reduced browsing pressure and helped woody plants recover where conditions suited growth.
Hunting elk outside park boundaries
Human harvest can also affect elk numbers. When explaining changes in browsing, consider hunting alongside wolf predation, other predators, weather and water availability.

From wolves and elk to plants, beavers and rivers

Elk are an important prey species in Yellowstone. When wolves were absent, heavy elk browsing limited the growth of some young willows and other woody plants. After wolves returned, changes in elk numbers and behaviour reduced browsing in some places. Where soil moisture and water conditions also suited growth, some plants recovered. Taller shrubs can support birds, and willows provide food and building material for beavers. Beaver dams can create ponds, retain water and sediment, and support wetland species. Roots can help stabilise some banks. These effects differ among stream reaches, so a local change should not be generalised to every river.

Why the response varied

Coyotes can compete with wolves and feed on small mammals. A change in coyote numbers or behaviour could affect rabbits or rodents, but their populations also respond to vegetation, weather and other predators. Hunting outside the park, other predators and changing climate also influence elk. If wolves declined again, reduced predation might allow more elk browsing, but the result would depend on those other pressures. Compare measurements of elk, browsing, woody growth and stream conditions at multiple sites. This makes a stronger explanation than assuming every change after reintroduction had the same cause.

Pollution across Earth’s systems

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

From source to impact

Pollution occurs when substances or forms of energy enter the environment at levels that can cause harm. Air, water and soil pollution usually involve particles, gases, liquids or other materials. Noise and light pollution involve unwanted sound or artificial light. Visual pollution refers to features such as excessive signs or rubbish that reduce a place’s visual quality.

Four spheres, connected pathways

In the atmosphere, wind moves smoke and gases. In the hydrosphere, currents and runoff move pollutants through rivers and oceans. In the lithosphere, substances can bind to soil or seep towards groundwater. In the biosphere, organisms can take in pollutants through breathing, eating or contact. For some persistent contaminants, concentration can build up in an organism over time and increase at higher levels of the food web.

Compare the type, scale and duration of harm

A local fuel spill can cause intense pollution for a short time. Diffuse fertiliser runoff may occur across a wide catchment after many storms. Persistent chemicals can remain in sediment or organisms for much longer than the event that introduced them. Good descriptions name the pollutant, explain its pathway and state where and when an effect is likely. This helps match a strategy to the problem.

Distinguish accumulation from magnification

Bioaccumulation happens when a contaminant builds up in one organism over time. Biomagnification happens when concentrations increase at higher feeding levels for certain persistent contaminants. A small fish can take up a contaminant from its surroundings and food. A predator that eats many small fish can then receive more of it. Different pollutants behave in different ways, so evidence about the substance matters.

Sources, pathways and management

Human activities that contribute

Burning fuel in vehicles or factories
Exhaust or smoke releases gases and particles into the atmosphere. Wind can carry them away from the source, and some pollutants later settle on land or water.
Applying fertiliser or leaving construction soil exposed
Rain can move nutrients and sediment into drains and streams. Downstream effects can include algal growth or reduced light.
Dumping waste or allowing storage tanks to leak
Pollutants can enter soil, move with runoff or seep into groundwater. People and organisms can then encounter them far from the original site.

Compare the six types of pollution

Air pollution includes particles and gases in the atmosphere. Water and soil pollution involve contaminants in waterways or on land. Noise pollution involves unwanted sound, light pollution involves excessive or misdirected artificial light at night, and visual pollution affects the appearance or experience of a place. Several can arise from one source. A busy road can emit fine particles, produce noise, require lighting and generate runoff that carries sediment or other contaminants into drains.

Interrupt the pathway and track the result

For an industrial spill, preventing leaks addresses the source, barriers and containment can limit movement, and monitoring checks whether contaminants reached soil or water. At a broader scale, vehicle emission standards and cleaner transport target airborne pollutants. Waste collection reduces dumping, and water treatment and vegetation buffers can reduce certain waterborne pollutants. A single intervention may move pollution between spheres. For example, material captured in a treatment plant still needs safe handling. Compare the relevant conditions upstream and downstream, or before and after action, and record weather and sampling conditions.

What makes air unhealthy?

Air pollution includes fine particles and gases. PM2.5 refers to particles no more than 2.5 micrometres across in aerodynamic diameter. Sources include fossil-fuel combustion, wood smoke, bushfires, industrial activity and some forms of dust. Ground-level ozone can form when gases from vehicles and industry react in sunlight. Wind, rainfall and still conditions affect whether pollution disperses or builds up.

Effects and responses

Fine particles can reach deep into the lungs. Repeated exposure can contribute to respiratory and cardiovascular problems. Some air pollutants also damage leaves, reduce visibility or affect soils and waterways after deposition. Responses include cleaner energy, factory emission controls, cleaner vehicles, improved public transport and limits on open burning.

Reading a map or graph

A live air-quality map is a snapshot. Compare locations at the same time with the same index, and check which pollutant is driving the reading. An annual PM2.5 comparison uses a concentration in micrograms per cubic metre and smooths daily spikes. Differences may reflect emissions, weather, fires or differences in monitoring coverage. Think about who is exposed

A person living near a busy road may experience different conditions from someone living in the same city near a large park. Pollution can also move from one region to another on the wind. Water pollution

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

What enters waterways?

Sediment, fertilisers, pesticides, sewage, oil and plastics can enter rivers, lakes and oceans. A point source is identifiable, such as a leaking pipe. Diffuse pollution comes from multiple locations, such as runoff from farms and streets across a catchment. Downstream impacts can occur far from the original source.

Nutrients and oxygen

Extra nitrogen or phosphorus can encourage rapid growth of algae, a process linked to eutrophication. A dense bloom may block sunlight. As algae die, decomposers use dissolved oxygen. If oxygen becomes low enough, fish and other organisms can die. Sewage may also introduce pathogens, while sediment can smother habitats and increase turbidity.

What water quality measurements show

Turbidity measures how cloudy water is. Dissolved oxygen measures oxygen available in the water. Nutrient concentrations indicate how much of substances such as nitrogen or phosphorus are present. Each describes a different condition. Clear water can contain dissolved pollutants or pathogens. In an investigation, use several indicators and compare places and dates under similar conditions.

From farm to creek after rain

Rain running across bare soil can carry sediment and nutrients into a creek. At a bend farther downstream, fine sediment can settle and cover gravel used by aquatic organisms. A vegetated buffer slows some runoff and traps some sediment before it enters the channel. Reducing fertiliser losses at the source can limit dissolved nutrients that might otherwise pass through the buffer. Combining strategies addresses more than one pathway.

Why downstream readings can differ

Fast-moving, shallow water can mix with air, increasing dissolved oxygen. Warm, slow-moving water can hold less oxygen, and decomposition can reduce it further. If two reaches have different oxygen levels, compare water temperature, flow and signs of nutrient pollution before choosing the most likely explanation.

Causes, effects and treatment

Human activities that contribute

Applying fertiliser or leaving paddocks and building sites bare
Rain can carry nutrients and sediment into waterways. Extra nutrients can feed algal growth, while sediment increases turbidity and can cover habitat.
Allowing sewers or septic systems to leak or overflow
Wastewater can carry pathogens and nutrients to creeks, beaches or groundwater. Leaks and overflows may affect different locations during wet and dry conditions.
Washing pollutants off urban surfaces
Stormwater can carry oil, tyre particles and litter from roads and car parks to drains. The pollution reaches downstream waterways unless intercepted.

From source to impact

A leaking sewage pipe is a point source because it can be traced to a particular location. Runoff from many properties is diffuse. In waterways, sediment can block light and bury habitats, while fertilisers can promote algal growth. After an algal bloom, decomposition can reduce dissolved oxygen and cause fish kills. Sewage may carry pathogens, which is why contact with contaminated water can harm people even if it looks clear. Industrial chemicals or oil can affect wildlife, and the impact depends on the pollutant, its concentration and how long organisms are exposed.

Management and monitoring

Sewer repairs and treatment help stop sewage entering waterways. Farmers can reduce losses by applying fertiliser to match crop needs, keeping soil covered and protecting streamside vegetation. Councils can treat or slow stormwater before it reaches creeks. Management should be checked against what it was designed to improve. Compare dissolved oxygen and nutrient concentrations if the concern is algal growth, sediment or turbidity if runoff is muddy, and microbial indicators if the issue is swimming water. Repeat sampling under comparable conditions and after rain.

Soil pollution

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Sources and movement

Soil pollution occurs when harmful substances accumulate in the ground. Possible sources include leaking fuel, historic industry, contaminated fill, dumped waste and some agricultural chemicals. Contaminants can attach to soil particles, be carried away by erosion or move with water into groundwater. What happens depends on the chemical, soil type and water movement.

Effects and investigation

Some contaminants harm soil organisms, reduce plant growth or enter food chains. For people, exposure depends on how the land is used and whether contaminated dust, water or produce reaches them. Geographers compare land-use history, likely sources and mapped sampling locations. Laboratory testing helps identify contaminants and their concentrations. A soil pH test can describe acidity but cannot establish whether soil contains lead or other pollutants.

Why land use changes exposure

A contaminated site beneath a sealed car park may present a different exposure risk if it is converted into a community garden. Digging can expose buried material, and gardeners can come into contact with soil or dust. This is why land-use history and the proposed future use matter when investigating contamination. Soil can also affect other systems if material leaves the site in stormwater or enters groundwater.

What a sampling map can tell you

Imagine concentrations are greatest near a former workshop and decrease downhill. This pattern suggests a possible source and pathway. To strengthen the interpretation, compare with sites uphill and farther away, check drainage and sample repeatedly if conditions vary. A pattern is stronger evidence when it fits the land-use history and the way the substance moves.

Identifying and managing contamination

Human activities that contribute

Spilling fuel or leaking it from underground tanks
Fuel can enter soil and some compounds can move into groundwater. Conditions such as soil type and drainage affect the pathway.
Using or disposing of hazardous materials on industrial land
Historic industrial activity or poor storage can leave contaminants in soil. Construction can expose buried material and move it as dust or contaminated fill.
Dumping waste or using contaminated fill
Uncontrolled disposal can introduce metals or other harmful substances. Flooding or erosion can then carry contaminated particles elsewhere.

Sources, movement and impacts

Fuel leaks, industrial waste, contaminated fill and inappropriate disposal can introduce pollutants. Soil contamination is different from soil erosion, although erosion can move contaminated particles. Some contaminants can move in dust or dissolve into groundwater, while others remain bound to soil. Effects depend on the substance and exposure pathway. A contaminant may affect plant growth or soil organisms, or reach people through dust, water or produce under particular conditions.

Test the hypothesis and compare responses

Start with the site history and map likely sources, slopes and drainage. A sampling plan can compare likely pathways with sites farther away or uphill, while laboratory tests identify the particular contaminants. Capping can isolate contaminated material and limit contact, but it needs maintenance. Removal or treatment can address material directly, though it may cost more and require specialist management. Where groundwater carries pollutants, interception and treatment can help contain movement. Compare concentrations over time at locations inside and beyond the affected area.

Noise pollution

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

When sound becomes pollution

Noise pollution is unwanted or excessive sound that interferes with people or wildlife. Common sources include road traffic, aircraft, trains, construction and industry. Sound is often measured in decibels, a logarithmic scale. A small numerical change can represent a substantial change in sound intensity. The time, duration and repetition of sound also affect its impact.

Effects on people and wildlife

Repeated noise can interrupt sleep and make concentration or communication harder. Some animals rely on calls to find mates, warn of predators or defend territory. Traffic noise can mask these calls. Underwater, vessel and construction noise can interfere with communication or change how some marine species use a habitat.

Exposure varies with place and time

Traffic noise can rise during peak travel times, while construction may produce short peaks during the day. At night, a similar noise may be more disruptive because people are trying to sleep and background sound is lower. Walls and buildings can block some sound, while a direct line of sight to a road can increase exposure. Distance, barriers and the timing of activity help explain differences between neighbouring locations.

A comparison you can design

To compare noise near a road and inside a park, record measurements for the same length of time and note traffic, weather and unusual events. Repeat at similar times on more than one day. Describe both the typical level and unusual peaks. If a siren passes only at one site, record it separately rather than treating it as representative of everyday conditions.

Read a noise survey

Suppose readings beside a road are consistently higher than readings behind a sound barrier during the same peak period. This supports the idea that the barrier limits exposure at the measured site. Check whether distance, traffic and the measuring position were comparable before deciding how much of the difference the barrier explains.

Measuring and reducing exposure

Human activities that contribute

Operating road, rail and aircraft transport
Traffic creates repeated noise along corridors. Aircraft can cause short peaks in areas beneath flight paths.
Using construction or industrial machinery
Equipment can generate loud or persistent sound. The timing, duration, distance and barriers affect how much reaches homes, schools or habitats.
Running amplified events near homes or wildlife habitat
Loudspeakers can increase noise beyond the venue, especially when they are directed towards nearby receptors or operate late.

The source, the sound path and the receptor

Road, rail and aircraft noise can differ in timing. A road may produce a sustained background level, while aircraft and construction can create short peaks. Both level and duration matter. Where noise masks an animal’s calls, it can interfere with communication or change where that animal feeds and breeds. In people, the effect depends on exposure and timing. A busy road near homes may be especially disruptive at night.

Match the technique to the path

Quieter equipment and vehicles reduce noise at the source. A barrier or building layout can interrupt the path. Setbacks and limits on operating hours can reduce exposure at homes, schools or wildlife habitat. To compare two locations, use the same measuring device, time interval and height, and record traffic, wind and unusual events. Decibels are logarithmic, so a change in decibels does not represent the same percentage change in sound level. Compare results with the same measure and averaging period.

Light pollution

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Where artificial light goes

Light pollution is excessive, poorly directed or unnecessary artificial light at night. Skyglow is the brightening of the night sky over a populated area. Glare is intense light that makes seeing harder. Light trespass occurs when illumination reaches an area where it is unwanted. Clutter occurs when many competing lights make a place hard to read.

Effects on wildlife and people

Artificial light can alter the timing of feeding, migration and breeding. Hatchling sea turtles may be drawn towards land-based lights rather than heading towards the sea. Insects can gather around bright lights, changing feeding opportunities for some animals. Night lighting can disturb sleep, and skyglow reduces visibility of stars.

Match the solution to the problem

A shield can reduce light spilling above or beside a fixture. A timer can reduce lighting during periods when an area is unused. Reducing brightness or using a more suitable light colour can reduce some ecological impacts. Changes must fit the place. Lighting at a busy pedestrian crossing has a different purpose from decorative lighting left on overnight beside a beach.

Investigate lighting around a nesting beach

Map bright lights visible from the beach and note their direction, height and timing. Compare observations from similar positions on different nights. Moonlight, cloud and tide can change what is visible, so record those conditions. A before-and-after comparison could test whether shielding reduced light reaching the beach. Changes in hatchling movement would require additional observations and specialist monitoring.

Compare a map with a ground observation

A satellite image mainly detects light that reaches its sensor. A resident’s observation can show glare or unwanted light at ground level. A lighting change may improve one of these measures more than the other, so match the evidence to the problem under investigation.

Identifying and managing artificial light

Human activities that contribute

Installing unshielded streetlights or floodlights
Light directed upward contributes to skyglow. Light directed sideways can produce glare or spill into homes and habitats.
Leaving commercial signs or buildings lit overnight
Lighting after closing extends artificial brightness into hours when people and many species would otherwise experience darkness.
Building lit roads or developments close to nesting beaches
Light from nearby buildings, paths or vehicles can reach the beach. Some turtle hatchlings may be drawn towards the artificial light and away from the sea.

Why lighting changes can affect wildlife

Light can spill upward as skyglow, into nearby areas as trespass or directly into someone’s field of view as glare. Clutter can make lights harder to interpret. New developments and more lighting can brighten some locations, while changes to lighting schedules can reduce light in others. The direction, brightness, colour and timing of light all matter. Near a turtle nesting beach, landward lights can draw some hatchlings away from the sea. Different animals respond to different wavelengths and conditions.

Use less light where and when possible

Keep sensitive places naturally dark where practical. When lighting is needed, shield it and direct it at the task, use the lowest suitable brightness and select timing and colour appropriate to the location. Timers or movement sensors can limit how long a light operates. A satellite image can help identify bright areas, while field observations can reveal where light reaches a beach or home. Compare readings before and after a change from the same places and at similar times.

Data and research credit · Australian national light pollution guidelines for wildlife.

Visual pollution

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

How places look and function

Visual pollution describes features people consider intrusive, distracting or harmful to a landscape’s visual quality. Examples can include cluttered signs, dumped waste, neglected infrastructure or poorly placed billboards. Views vary with cultural values, location and the function of a place. A bright sign may be expected in a shopping area and considered intrusive next to a scenic lookout.

Effects on people and environments

Excess signs can obscure landmarks, interrupt views or make navigation harder when several messages compete for attention. Litter reduces visual amenity and may also create a physical hazard to wildlife. An oversized sign can have a visual impact even if it has little direct effect on wildlife. Illuminated signs may add both visual and light pollution.

Separate measurable features and opinions

Counting signs, measuring their size or photographing blocked views provides observable evidence. A survey can show whether people feel the street is cluttered or hard to navigate. People may rate the same feature differently, so report what each method measures. A detailed answer can combine both kinds of evidence rather than relying on one impression.

A planning example

A main street needs signs for businesses, directions and public transport. A council might group directional signs, limit illuminated advertising and keep important sightlines clear. A successful plan can improve navigation while allowing shops to be identified. Compare photographs and ask different users whether the changes made signs easier to find. A lower count alone does not prove that the street works better.

Consider the scale of a response

A street clean-up can improve the view quickly, while better bins and more reliable collection may prevent litter from reappearing. Survey the same route several weeks later to see whether the benefit lasts and whether waste has moved to a different location.

Investigating visual change

Human activities that contribute

Adding many large or poorly placed signs
Advertising and signs can compete for attention, block views and make useful directions harder to spot in streets or near scenic areas.
Dumping rubbish or failing to maintain public spaces
Visible waste and neglected infrastructure can change how people experience a place. Loose litter can also wash into waterways.
Siting infrastructure without considering important views
Poorly positioned barriers or equipment can obscure landmarks and reduce the visual quality of a public space. The impact depends on how people use and value the setting.

Impacts depend on the setting

Too many signs, poorly sited billboards, visible litter and neglected infrastructure can obscure views or make directions difficult to find. A sign that is useful in one place may be intrusive in another. Visual pollution can overlap with other problems: dumped plastic can affect wildlife, and an illuminated sign can also cause light trespass. A study can record the physical feature, such as the number and placement of signs, and separately record how different people experience it.

Manage the problem and compare viewpoints

Planning controls can guide the size, location and brightness of signs. A clean-up can remove litter immediately, while better bins and collection can reduce repeated dumping. Use fixed-point photographs, counts along the same route and surveys of different groups. Compare results before and after the change at similar times. A fall in sign numbers can show that the rule affected the street, but feedback on navigation and visibility is needed to show whether people find the space easier to use.

Topographic maps and contours

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Reading the shape of the land

A topographic map represents features such as hills, valleys, rivers and roads on a flat surface. Contour lines join points at the same elevation above a reference level, usually mean sea level. The contour interval is the vertical difference between adjacent contours. Close contours show a steep slope because elevation changes quickly across a short horizontal distance. Widely spaced contours show a gentler slope.

Applying the map

A steep slope can speed runoff and make construction difficult. A valley floor may have access to water but be exposed to flooding. Maps reveal topography, but decisions may also need rainfall, vegetation and flood-history evidence.

Read scale and relief together

Elevation is height above a reference level. Relief is the difference in elevation between high and low points in an area. On a 1:25,000 map, 1 centimetre represents 250 metres on the ground. A route that measures 4 centimetres therefore represents 1 kilometre horizontally. Actual walking distance can be greater if the route bends or crosses uneven ground. Compare both distance and steepness when choosing a route.

Estimate an elevation between contours

If a point lies between the 120 m and 140 m contours, its elevation is between those values. A point halfway across the gap may be estimated at about 130 m if the slope is reasonably even. A spot height is more precise when one is marked. A close set of contour lines represents steepness. The contour interval remains the same even when lines appear closer together on the page.

Contour patterns and profiles

Recognise slopes, hills, valleys and saddles

A steep slope has contours close together because elevation changes quickly over a short horizontal distance. A gentler slope has wider spacing. Closed contours whose heights increase toward the centre indicate a hill. Where contours cross a stream valley, the bends commonly point upstream toward higher ground. A saddle is a lower area between two higher points. On a profile, a single hill shows one rise and fall, while two hills joined by a saddle show a dip between their peaks. The contour interval stays the same between adjacent standard contours even when their horizontal spacing varies.

Draw and check a cross-section

Place the edge of a strip of paper along the chosen line. Mark each place where it crosses a contour and label the height. Transfer the marks to a graph, with distance along the line on the horizontal axis and elevation on the vertical axis. Plot each height and join the points to show the land profile. Add a title and units. A steeper section of the profile should correspond to closer contours along the map line. A vertical scale that stretches heights more than the horizontal scale stretches distances can make the slope look steeper than it is.

Four-figure area and six-figure grid references

ReadingIn depthReal-world dataWorked examplePractice questions

Eastings first, northings second

Vertical grid lines are eastings and their values increase eastwards. Horizontal grid lines are northings and their values increase northwards. Start with the bottom-left corner of the square containing the feature. Read the easting first and then the northing. Think “along the corridor, then up the stairs”.

Area reference and grid reference

A four-figure area reference (AR) identifies a grid square. For a feature in the square immediately east of easting 24 and north of northing 63, the AR is 2463. A six-figure grid reference (GR) locates a point within the square. Divide the square into tenths, estimate how far the point lies east of the left line and north of the bottom line, then write three digits for each. At six tenths east and four tenths north, the reference is 246634.

Check your reference

Use the feature itself, not its label, when reading the map. Do not combine the tenths digits at the end of a four-figure reference. Add each tenths digit to its own coordinate before joining the two coordinates.

Work from the feature, not the label

A campsite symbol may have a label printed in a nearby square to keep the map readable. Take the reference from the symbol. For a point in the square east of line 25 and north of line 62, the four-figure area reference is 2562. If the point sits three tenths east and seven tenths north, write the easting as 253 and the northing as 627. Join them to form 253627.

Test your reference in reverse

Split 232628 into 232 and 628. The first two digits of each part identify the square: easting 23, northing 62. The final digits tell you to move two tenths right and eight tenths up. Plot that location to check the original reference. A four-figure reference is suitable for describing which square contains a broad feature. A six-figure reference is useful for locating a specific point, such as a sampling site.

Reading and checking references

Move across, then up

Eastings are the vertical lines and their values increase from west to east. Northings are the horizontal lines and their values increase from south to north. Start at the south-west corner of the square containing the feature. For a four-figure area reference, read the easting on the left edge and then the northing along the bottom edge. The answer identifies a square. A six-figure grid reference adds one digit to each coordinate to show the position within the square.

Work both forwards and backwards

The Wattle Creek lookout lies in square 2463, six tenths of a square east of line 24 and four tenths north of line 63. Its grid reference is 246634. To reverse the reference, split it into 246 and 634. The first two digits of each part identify the square, and the last digit of each part gives the movement within it. For a campsite, read from the centre of the map symbol. For a wide area such as a woodland, use an area reference if the question asks for the square containing it. A six-figure reference identifies a much smaller position, subject to the scale and accuracy of the map.
